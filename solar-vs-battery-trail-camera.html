Solar vs Battery Trail Camera — 5-Year Cost, Battery Count and Which to Buy

A battery camera costs less on day one. A solar camera costs less on day 300, when you are not climbing a ridge to swap eight cells in the rain.

The solar versus battery decision is usually framed as a price question, but the real variable is access. If the camera sits behind your house, swapping batteries twice a year is a five-minute job and the cheaper battery model wins. If the camera is a kilometre off a trail in a lease property, every battery change is an afternoon — and that is before you count the images sitting unread on the card for weeks. This guide compares the two power systems on battery count, placement limits and five-season running cost, using the published specifications of the Arvelix Vision range.

The One-Line Answer

Buy solar when the camera is hard to reach or runs year-round. Buy battery when the camera is close to home, used seasonally, or shaded by heavy canopy.

  • Solar models (PR903DW, PR903G, H1W, H2G) pair a 5200mAh or 7800mAh built-in battery with a solar panel. Most also accept an extra 8×AA set as backup.
  • Battery models (PR1600, PR700, PR8000, PR801LTE, PR1200 and others) run on 8×AA cells rated for up to six months of standby, with no panel to place or keep clear.

How the Two Power Systems Actually Differ

Battery cameras treat the AA cells as the only power source. Capacity is fixed: when the cells are flat, the camera stops. That makes runtime predictable but also limited, and it makes placement a logistical decision — you have to be willing to visit.

Solar cameras use a two-part system: a built-in rechargeable battery (5200mAh or 7800mAh in our models) topped up by a solar panel, with AA cells as a reserve. The panel does not need direct noon sun to contribute, but it does need daylight reaching it. This is why solar cameras are usually mounted with the panel facing the open sky, which sometimes forces a compromise between the ideal detection angle and the ideal charging angle.

That trade-off is the part most buyers miss. A solar camera in deep forest canopy behaves like a battery camera with a permanent parasitic load — the panel cannot keep up, and the backup cells do all the work.

When a Battery Model Is Still the Right Answer

Solar is not universally better, and pushing it into the wrong site creates its own problems. A battery model remains the better choice when:

  • The site is shaded or the panel would face away from the light — a panel that cannot charge turns a solar camera into a heavier battery camera.
  • You only run the camera for a season. One set of AA cells rated for up to six months can cover an entire hunting season, leaving no time for the solar premium to pay back.
  • The camera is within easy reach. If swapping cells costs five minutes twice a year, there is nothing for solar to save.
  • You want the lowest upfront cost per unit — relevant when equipping a dozen positions at once.

Battery models in the range include the PR1600, PR700, PR8000, PR801LTE and PR1200, all on the same 8×AA platform.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a solar trail camera worth the extra cost?
It depends on access. For remote sites the value is not only the cells saved but the trips avoided. If the camera sits within easy reach, a standard 8×AA model usually costs less overall.

How long does a solar camera last without sunlight?
Built-in batteries are 5200mAh (PR903DW, PR903G) or 7800mAh (H1W, H2G). Most models also accept an extra 8×AA backup set, so an overcast week or a snow-covered panel does not take the camera offline.

Do solar trail cameras still need AA batteries?
Not as the main source. The PR903DW, PR903G and H1W accept an extra 8×AA set as backup; the H2G runs on its 7800mAh built-in battery and panel only. Backup cells are what carry the camera through extended bad weather.

How many AA batteries does a camera use per year?
An 8×AA camera rated for six months of standby works out to roughly two sets per year — about 16 cells. Real use rises with trigger frequency, night-shot share and cold weather.

Which is better for a remote property?
Solar, despite the higher upfront cost — especially combined with cellular so you also avoid image-retrieval trips. The PR903G and H2G combine both.

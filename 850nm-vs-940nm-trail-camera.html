850nm vs 940nm Night Vision — What Trail Camera Buyers Should Know in 2026

Both wavelengths are invisible in daylight and both work in total darkness — but 850nm reaches further with a faint red glow, while 940nm stays unseen and needs far more LEDs to do it.

When buyers compare trail cameras, the night vision specification is often the one they understand least. Two cameras can both claim "up to 20 metres night vision" while using completely different infrared wavelengths — one with four LEDs, the other with forty-six. The wavelength decides whether an animal notices the camera, how far the illumination actually reaches, and how much of the battery is spent after dark. This guide breaks down the real difference between 850nm and 940nm, using the specifications of Arvelix Vision models as worked examples.

The One-Line Answer

Choose 940nm when stealth matters — hunting, wary game, covert monitoring. Choose 850nm when reach and efficiency matter — property security, farm surveillance and long-range identification.

  • 850nm emits a faint red glow that is visible to the naked eye in complete darkness. Sensors are efficient at this wavelength, so a handful of LEDs covers a long distance.
  • 940nm sits further outside the visible range. The glow is essentially undetectable in normal use, but sensors are much less efficient at 940nm, so the same distance requires many more LEDs.

How Infrared Night Vision Actually Works

At night a trail camera removes the infrared-blocking filter from its sensor and lights the scene with infrared LEDs. Because image sensors remain sensitive beyond the visible spectrum, the camera records a black-and-white image of a scene that appears pitch black to a human.

Why 850nm is more efficient. At 850 nanometres the sensor still responds strongly, so each LED delivers a large amount of usable illumination for relatively little power. This is why a 850nm camera can be built with only two to four emitters.

Why 940nm needs more of everything. At 940 nanometres the sensor's response drops substantially. To produce the same usable brightness, the manufacturer must fit many more diodes — which is why 940nm models in the Arvelix Vision range carry 34 to 46 emitters instead of two to four.

The practical consequence: two cameras rated for the same night distance are not built the same way, and the one with more LEDs consumes more power after dark. That is a battery-life consideration, not just a technical footnote.

Which Animals React to the Red Glow

Many mammals are more sensitive to red and near-infrared light than humans are, which is why 850nm cameras have a reputation for spooking wary game. Field reports from hunters repeatedly describe deer, wild boar and predators reacting to the faint glow of an 850nm unit — particularly when the camera is newly placed and the animal is already alert.

Two practical qualifications matter here:

  • Reaction is not universal. Animals respond differently depending on species, how accustomed they are to the location, and whether the camera is mounted above their natural line of sight. A well-positioned 850nm camera can run for months without an obvious reaction.
  • If you cannot risk it, do not gamble. For trophy hunting, research plots or any situation where one spooked animal ruins the season, a 940nm no-glow model removes the variable entirely. The trade-off is cost and battery draw, not image quality.

What 940nm Really Costs You in Battery

Because 940nm illumination needs many more emitters, night photography draws more power. In practice this appears as a shorter battery life in high-traffic locations rather than a dramatic failure.

  • A camera triggered occasionally at night will show little difference between the two wavelengths.
  • A busy night-time location — a feeder, a gate or a trail junction — can trigger the LEDs dozens of times per night. That is where the higher LED count of a 940nm model starts to matter.
  • On both Arvelix Vision platforms the battery spec is 8×AA with up to six months of standby, so battery type and quality matter as much as wavelength. Rechargeable or low-drain cells are worth specifying for 940nm units in busy locations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 940nm better than 850nm?
940nm is better for stealth — nearly invisible to the human eye and far less likely to disturb wary game. 850nm is better for reach and efficiency: a few 850nm LEDs deliver illumination that needs 34–46 940nm LEDs. Choose 940nm for hunting, 850nm for property security and long-range identification.

Why do 940nm cameras need so many more LEDs?
Image sensors are less sensitive at 940nm than at 850nm, so each diode produces less usable illumination. To reach a comparable distance, 940nm models use far more emitters — the Arvelix Vision PR1600 carries 46pcs of 940nm no-glow infrared, while the 850nm PR8000 uses 4pc for the same 20m rating.

Can animals see the red glow of an 850nm camera?
Many mammals are more sensitive to red and near-infrared light than humans are, and hunters frequently report deer and other wary species noticing 850nm LEDs. If avoiding detection matters more than maximum range, choose a 940nm no-glow model.

Does the wavelength affect daytime photo quality?
No. Infrared LEDs only operate at night. Daylight image and video quality depend on sensor, lens and firmware — two cameras on the same platform produce identical daytime images regardless of LED wavelength.

Which wavelength should a distributor stock?
Most stock both. Hunting markets — especially the US and Western Europe — favour 940nm no-glow for stealth, while farm and property buyers usually accept 850nm because it gives more reach for the money.
